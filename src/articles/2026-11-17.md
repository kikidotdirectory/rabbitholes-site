i might need to look up the distribution of word counts across all of Wikipedia because at some point i would like *something* to get myself started on. or maybe i'll start doing rerolls but i would rather avoid that because then what i read may become biased by what i find interesting.

maybe it's lucky that i got a tiny article today— i don't feel like i have much in the way of energy for curiosity. maybe a byproduct of overworking. 

I wonder how people put together these articles. Is it just whatever information comes up when looking it up online? How does Wikipedia treat primary sources? Given that this and [[2026-11-15 | Karczmiska]] are more remote locations[^1], I assume that would correspond to meaning that there is less information about it. Maybe less people go there, less traffic in general. My understanding of remote locations is that people don't tend to come and go; they either stay a long time or leave and not return. Perhaps that's just my bias from only living in very densely populated areas.

I think about how many older cultures persist through oral... retellings(? my mind can't find the word for it) but those aren't legible to the Wikipedia sources because anyone can edit it and so the individual's own voice is devalued. I read *In Praise of Shadows* yesterday and what lingers with me most is how the technology that we use is shaped by a Western perspective in a Western culture to serve Western ideals and meet Western needs. Cultures that differ from it are not legible to the Western understanding and are not able to coexist with it. 

I don't have the energy to dig deeply into this but one thing I did read is how [Kerala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerala), the state this village is apart of, is officially named "Keralam" after a "long-standing demand to change the official English name of the state" which was "effected officially on 25 August 2026 after it was notified by the Ministry of Home affairs of the Government of India."[^2] Why is it then, if the name is officially changed and there are sources for it, that the name for the article is still Kerala? 

The ideal of Wikipedia is to be this impartial beacon of information but it is marred by the same biases as everything else of its nature, the biases of how its ideals formed, the biases from the decisions made in creating its system.

It reminds me of the [Unicode Consortium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicode_Consortium), an organization whose purpose is to maintain the Unicode standard, the language machines speak so that all humans can speak to another. Its purpose is to serve every population in the world, so I don't think it would be unreasonable to think that it would serve that mission to make efforts to diversify the voices making these decisions. However, 'the [composition of the Consortium’s members, directors, and officers](http://www.unicode.org/consortium/directors.html)...are comprised largely of white men (and a few white women) whose first language was either English or another European language.'[^3] As of today, the Unicode Technical Committee (UTC), the board that decide whether new characters will be encoded, has 8 full members: Adobe, Airbnb, Apple, Google, Meta, Microsoft, Salesforce and Translated.[^4] Of those 8 members, 7 are founded and headquartered in the USA and the last in Italy. Hardly diverse. However, once you understand that full membership is only acquired by a sum of $50,000 USD, it makes more sense. And when you look at the diversity of the board, it becomes much less surprising (though no less disappointing) that this same organization wanted to [unify all many Eastern scripts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CJK_Unified_Ideographs), identified as 'ideographs.'[^5] As a reminder, we have over a thousand unicode points reserved for [Egyptian hieroglyphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_Hieroglyphs_(Unicode_block)).[^6]

Today's post is not really about the article, but I suppose it is a rabbithole nonetheless, so mission accomplished (i guess?).
